I truly admire when someone masters their craft—when they know every detail and can speak about it for hours without preparation or effortlessly tackle any situation, resolving issues on the spot. There’s a certain charm to it. Every time I witness such mastery, a part of me feels inspired to return home and refine my own tools and craft, whatever my goal might be at that moment.  

Many authors have discussed the hidden secrets to achieving mastery, from renowned writers to the average YouTuber recycling clichéd advice and offering "all the secrets" if we just follow their process (and, of course, buy their $20.99 course).  

It seems that most people perceive the path to mastery in extremes. Some believe in Malcolm Gladwell’s 10,000-hour rule, popularized in *Outliers*, or Robert Greene’s *Mastery*, which emphasizes similar themes.  

Others lean heavily on focus and time management, as Cal Newport suggests in *Deep Work*. For him, it’s all about consistency, intense focus, and avoiding the distraction of passion. This approach is undeniably helpful for day-to-day productivity and long-term career planning.  

On the other hand, David Epstein, in *Range*, advocates for the opposite: broadening your skill set and generalizing your talents. He emphasizes learning across disciplines, arguing that a wide spectrum of knowledge is what truly matters in the end.  

After giving this a lot of thought, I’ve concluded that the ideal approach lies somewhere in the middle—a "gray" approach. It’s neither entirely about specialization nor complete generalization. Instead, one should initially broaden their horizons carefully, exploring different areas, and then deep-dive into one or two skills to achieve mastery.  

As Oliver Burkeman notes in *Four Thousand Weeks*, the finitude of our time is the most crucial factor. We can’t do everything we love, but it’s far more rewarding to achieve depth in one area than to gather shallow knowledge about many. The hardest part is identifying the right focus—not driven purely by passion but a mix of knowledge, opportunity, and conviction that makes the investment worthwhile. Once you’ve found that topic, the journey begins.  

The real challenge, however, is knowing when to continue and when to stop. I highly recommend Professor Richard Hamming’s talk, *[You and Your Research](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=a1zDuOPkMSw)*. He advises asking yourself: *What is the most important problem in your field, and why aren’t you working on it?*  

I’ll revisit this topic in a couple of years, and I’m sure my perspective will evolve yet again. For now, I’ll leave you with this beautiful quote from Oliver Burkeman:  

> “We’ve been granted the mental capacities to make almost infinitely ambitious plans, yet practically no time at all to put them into action.”  
> *Oliver Burkeman, Four Thousand Weeks*
